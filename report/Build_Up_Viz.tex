\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We label every frame of the tactical video as home possession (\code{H}), away possession (\code{A}),
dead ball (\code{D}) or no answer (\code{X}). Two gradient-boosted heads estimate whether the ball is
alive and which team owns it, and a transformer-plus-LSTM owner head strengthens the second. A
four-state hidden semi-Markov model with minimum durations turns the per-frame probabilities into
whole possessions. Ball positions detected in the video fill part of the tracking's ball gaps.
Everything that is trained or tuned uses the seven public Sportec Open DFL matches, degraded to look
like automatic tracking; on the challenge game the model is only run. On the DFL bench, with each
match held out in turn, the model reaches spellF1~0.655, against 0.182 for the organisers' baseline.
The submitted version scores 0.671 on the public five-minute window (5--95\,\% bootstrap: 0.46--0.86).
\end{abstract}

\section{Approach}

\paragraph{Diagnosis.} spellF1 treats each possession, a maximal run of \code{H} or \code{A}, as one
object, matched by temporal IoU as in segmental F1 for action segmentation~\cite{lea2017tcn}. One
flicker inside a possession splits it into three objects, and \code{X} is replaced by the previous
label before scoring, so abstaining cannot help. The organisers' baseline predicts 131 possessions
on the public window, where there are 17. It also marks \code{D} whenever the ball is missing, which
happens on 41\,\% of the graded frames, mostly in live play. We concluded that the first gain comes
from \emph{modelling possession as a sequence}, not from seeing the ball better. Four principles
follow:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item separate \emph{is the ball alive?} from \emph{which team owns it?}, and join them in one
    sequence model with minimum durations;
  \item treat a missing ball as information: tracking loses it in aerial balls, crowds and
    stoppages, so it is not missing at random~\cite{rubin1976};
  \item train and tune only on external data, and only run the model on the challenge game;
  \item make the model exactly symmetric in the two teams, so it cannot learn a home bias.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work.} Rule-based detectors such as Vidal-Codina et al.~\cite{vidalcodina} assign possession and restarts from distances and player configurations, but
assume a reliable ball. Ball Radar~\cite{ballradar}, TranSPORTmer~\cite{transportmer} and
PathCRF~\cite{pathcrf} infer the ball or possession from the players alone, with set attention and
recurrent or CRF layers. PathCRF uses the same DFL matches and reports 92.4\,\% owner accuracy on
clean tracking. Our design borrows features from the first line and the architecture from the second.

\paragraph{Per-frame features (71).} They use team membership only, never track identity, so the
same code serves the bench and the challenge. Many are also averaged over centred windows of
0.5--10\,s that ignore missing values.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{37 symmetric} (unchanged when the teams are swapped): ball seen, time since and until
    seen, ball speed, distance outside the pitch of the last and next seen ball, nearest-player
    distance, players within 10\,m, duels, collective player speed (a dead-ball cue), touches (a
    velocity change above 4\,m/s within 2\,m of a player), and restart configurations after
    Vidal-Codina et al.: throw-in, corner, goal kick and kick-off.
  \item \emph{34 antisymmetric} (change sign): nearest home minus nearest away distance to the
    ball and to its last and next seen positions; territory; team speed; team of the last and next
    touch; 5\,s past-versus-future contrasts, which locate changes; set-piece team; and pitch
    control~\cite{fernandezBornn}, $2\sigma(I_\text{home}-I_\text{away})-1$, where each player's
    influence is a normal distribution stretched along the direction of motion.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Gradient-boosted heads.} Two \code{HistGradientBoostingClassifier} models (200
iterations, 31 leaves, L2 1), trained on every fifth frame of the degraded bench.
$q = P(\text{alive})$ uses the symmetric block and the absolute antisymmetric block.
$r = P(\text{home owns} \mid \text{alive})$ sees every live frame twice: as is with label $y$, and
with the antisymmetric block negated with label $1-y$. Swapping the teams therefore swaps the
prediction.

\paragraph{Neural owner head} (Figure~\ref{fig:owner}). At 5\,fps, a ball token (6 features,
including whether and when it was last seen) and up to 14 player tokens per team (7 features,
nearest to the ball first) are embedded by an MLP ($d=64$) with a team embedding. One transformer
layer (4 heads) attends over them. The ball token and each team's mean token feed a bidirectional
LSTM over 10\,s windows. The output $\tfrac12[f(x)-f(\mathrm{swap}\,x)]$ is exactly antisymmetric.
It was trained on the degraded bench only, on a Colab GPU: AdamW with one-cycle schedule, 15 epochs,
random team swaps and pitch mirroring. Three seeds are averaged, and the result is blended with $r$
in log-odds with weight $w = 0.75$, chosen out of fold. Every $w > 0$ won on all 7 matches.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/owner_nn.pdf}
  \caption{Neural owner head: attention over ball and player tokens per step, then a bidirectional
  LSTM over 10\,s. The output is antisymmetric in the teams by construction.}
  \label{fig:owner}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Sequence model.} A four-state hidden semi-Markov model (Figure~\ref{fig:hmm}) has live
states LH and LA, and dead states DH and DA, which remember the last owner and both emit \code{D}.
Emissions are the heads' scaled likelihoods, tempered by $\alpha$ because consecutive frames are
correlated, with $\pi$ the bench share of live frames:
\[
  e(\mathrm{LH}) = \alpha\Big[\log\tfrac{q}{\pi} + \log 2r'\Big],\quad
  e(\mathrm{LA}) = \alpha\Big[\log\tfrac{q}{\pi} + \log 2(1-r')\Big],\quad
  e(\mathrm{D\cdot}) = \alpha \log\tfrac{1-q}{1-\pi}.
\]
Each state is expanded into $m-1$ forced sub-states plus a geometric tail matching the bench mean
duration, the explicit-duration construction of~\cite{yu2010hsmm}. Transitions come from the bench
labels: a live state either switches team directly or goes dead, and a dead state restarts with
either team; everything else is forbidden. Viterbi~\cite{rabiner} decodes each half. A 60-point grid
with one match held out chose $\alpha = 0.75$, live $\geq 3$\,s and dead $\geq 4$\,s.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{figures/hmm.pdf}
  \caption{Four-state HSMM with minimum durations. A direct change of team is a live-to-live
  transition; after a dead ball, either team may restart.}
  \label{fig:hmm}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Smart Tagging.} Only \code{SET PIECES} windows are used, because a restart ends a dead
ball, the evidence tracking lacks most. They give three features: inside a window, signed time to its
centre, and its team when known. Each half's first \code{Kick Off (Start)} forces \code{D} before
it. Phase codes describe tactics, have no bench analogue, and are left out.

\paragraph{Ball fill from video.} WASB~\cite{wasb} runs inference only, with its public soccer
weights, inside the tracking's ball gaps of at least 1\,s plus 1\,s margins. At its $512\times288$
input the ball is about 1\,px, so each frame is cut into $2\times2$ overlapping $1024\times576$
tiles, as in SAHI~\cite{sahi}, making the ball about 1.9 times larger. Candidates are projected with
the homography and filtered by fixed rules: inside the pitch plus 2\,m, not within 1\,m of the
painted centre and penalty marks (Law~1~\cite{ifab}), the best one per frame, and supported by
another candidate within 0.4\,s at 40\,m/s or less. Gaps of up to 0.6\,s are joined linearly; the
tracking's ball is never overwritten. An automatic check fixed beforehand refuses the fusion if the
median distance to the tracking's ball, where both exist, exceeds 5\,m. It passed: $n = 3036$, median
1.15\,m. The ball becomes known on 66.3\,\% of graded frames instead of 59.3\,\%.

\section{Evaluation and results}

\paragraph{Protocol.} Each match is held out in turn and scored on two degraded copies, averaging
over control thresholds of 1, 2 and 3\,s (Section~\ref{sec:rules}). With seven folds, a mean has wide
error bars~\cite{varoquaux2018} and paired resampled tests are unreliable~\cite{dietterich}, so a
change is adopted only if it raises the mean \emph{and} wins on at least 5 of 7 matches.

\paragraph{Diagnostics.} Training with real pseudo-tags gave 0.590, against 0.528 without tags and
0.529 with the same tags shifted off the restarts, so the gain is the tags' information. Replacing
one part of v6 with the truth gives $+0.20$ for a perfect owner, $+0.07$ for a perfect ball (the
ceiling of vision) and $+0.05$ for a perfect alive/dead signal. The owner error lies mostly in the
cuts: the model predicts 345 possessions per match against 390, and 98\,\% of the merged ones are
direct home-to-away changes. Across matches, the share of missing ball predicts the score best
(correlation $-0.83$).

\paragraph{Public window.} It set no parameter. Scores, with 5--95\,\% bootstrap intervals: v6 0.645
(0.44--0.83), v7 0.650 (0.45--0.84), v8 0.671 (0.46--0.86). These differences are noise. v8 is
submitted because its fusion passed the check that the plan set, before the video run, as the
condition for using it. We disclose that all three had been scored on the window before the choice.

\noindent\textbf{Tried and dropped} (bench, adoption rule): relabelling whole possessions in a second

\section{Training data}
\label{sec:data}

The only labelled data is \textbf{Sportec Open DFL}~\cite{dfl} (figshare 28196177, CC~BY~4.0): 7
Bundesliga matches, TRACAB tracking at 25\,Hz, 1.0\,M frames in the halves, with per-frame
\code{BallStatus} and \code{BallPossession} flags from the official DFL position data (Sportec
Solutions). \textbf{None of it comes from the challenge game.} Labels are \code{D} where the ball is
dead and the owning team otherwise, then rewritten with the rule of Section~\ref{sec:rules}.

DFL tracking is corrected by hand, so each match is copied twice and degraded, with ranges fixed a
priori and never measured on the challenge tracking. The ball is removed on 20--60\,\% of frames in
gaps of 0.5--4\,s. Following~\cite{rubin1976}, gaps start more often when the ball is above 1.5\,m,
crowded (3 or more players within 3\,m) or dead. Players go missing on 2--15\,\% of frames; 5--50\,cm
noise, duplicate players and phantom balls are added. Smart Tagging is simulated from the DFL
restarts with 20--90\,\% recall, up to 2 false alarms per 10 minutes, up to 2\,s jitter and 20--60\,\%
unknown teams.

The neural owner head is trained on this data only. The WASB weights were trained by their authors on
ISSIA-CNR, offered for non-commercial research only; the weights declare no licence of their own and
we use them for inference only. We flag this as a licensing risk.

\section{Possession rules implemented}
\label{sec:rules}

The rules follow the challenge's definitions and Laws~8 and~9 of the Laws of the Game~\cite{ifab}.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item[Change of possession.] DFL gives the ball to whoever touches it; the challenge counts only
    \emph{controlled} possession. A live switch counts if the new team keeps the ball for at least
    2\,s, fixed a priori from ``a player keeps the ball for a few seconds''.
  \item[Loose balls, touches, deflections, lost aerial duels.] A switch shorter than 2\,s goes back
    to the team that had the ball.
  \item[Restarts.] After a dead ball the restarting team has possession immediately; set-piece tags
    and configurations say who restarts. Each half is dead until its kick-off window.
  \item[Durations.] Dead stretches last at least 4\,s and live possessions at least 3\,s.
  \item[Abstention.] \code{X} only outside the two halves, never on a frame that could be graded.
\end{description}

\section{Limitations and future work}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Long ball gaps.} The challenge tracking loses the ball for a median of about 10\,s and
    up to 221\,s, against 0.5--4\,s on the bench. Recalibrating would mean calibrating on the
    challenge data, so the bench probably overestimates our score. The video fills only 17\,\% of
    the gap frames.
  \item \textbf{Merged possessions.} Quick home-to-away exchanges are the main remaining error.
  \item \textbf{Annotator criterion.} DFL's flag and Metrica's annotators may disagree on duels and
    loose balls; the 2\,s rule is a proxy.
  \item \textbf{Tracking geometry.} 2.1\,\% of seen balls lie more than 2\,m outside the pitch, never
    seen on the bench.
  \item \textbf{Vision.} 27\,\% of accepted detections lie more than 3\,m from the tracked ball where
    both exist. The tiling was decided after the full-frame pass failed the check: a declared
    deviation, a single attempt with no values swept. The video was processed on Google Colab from
    the organisers' shared folder with no AI assistant enabled, which we flag as a risk. GPU
    inference may differ slightly across GPU models, hence the intermediate detection file.
  \item \textbf{Future work.} An owner head trained with a change-aware loss, a touch head trained on
    DFL events, and temporal tracking of ball candidates.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproduction}

Install Python 3.11 with \code{requirements-solution.txt}, put the challenge data in \code{data/},
download the DFL bench with the MD5-checked command in the README, and run:
\begin{verbatim}
python scripts/vision_fuse.py
python scripts/make_submission.py --team Build_Up_Viz \
       --ball-fill external/vision/ball_vision.npz
\end{verbatim}
The WASB detections are an intermediate file. We provide them on request, and the notebook
\code{notebooks/vision\_ball.ipynb} regenerates them. The pipeline is deterministic: a clean clone with empty caches
reproduces \code{Build\_Up\_Viz.csv} byte for byte in about 10 minutes on a CPU. Without
\code{--ball-fill} it needs only the challenge data and the DFL bench, and gives v7. Twenty-four unit
tests cover the label rule, the degradation, the team symmetry and the Viterbi decoder.
